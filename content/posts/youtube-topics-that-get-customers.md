> **Quick answer:** The fastest way to pick YouTube video topics that get you customers is to stop brainstorming and start listening to what people are already typing into the search bar. Validate every topic against real autocomplete and People Also Ask data before you film it, and sort ideas into buyer intent versus merely interesting before you commit a single hour to production. This guide covers the exact system.

If you are a founder posting on YouTube and wondering why some videos bring in real inquiries while others just sit there with decent views, the problem is almost never your camera, your editing, or your delivery. It is your topic selection. You are picking videos the way a hobbyist picks a subject for fun, when you should be picking them the way a researcher picks a question people are already trying to answer.

Here's the thing. YouTube does not reward the topic you find most interesting. It rewards the topic the most people are actively searching for, at the exact moment they need an answer. Those are frequently two completely different lists.

## Why "What Should I Post About" Is the Wrong Question

The question that gets founders stuck staring at a blank content calendar is "what should I post about this week." That question has no answer, because it starts from your imagination instead of your customer's search bar.

The right question is "what is my ideal customer typing into Google or YouTube right now, while they are dealing with the exact problem I solve." That question has dozens of concrete answers, because your customer has already told you what they are searching for. You just have not gone and looked yet.

I have had clients spend hours in a content brainstorm producing a list of clever, original video ideas, and every single one of them scored zero on actual search volume. Nobody was looking for any of it. Meanwhile the boring, obvious topic sitting right next to their business, the one that felt too basic to bother filming, was getting searched thousands of times a month. Obvious and boring is often exactly what converts, because obvious and boring is what people are actually stuck on.

## What Makes a Topic Buyer Intent Instead of Just Interesting?

Every topic falls into one of two buckets: buyer intent or merely interesting. Learn to sort your ideas into these two buckets before you film anything, because they produce completely different channels.

A buyer intent topic answers a question someone asks while they are actively trying to solve a problem they already know they have, usually with money attached to the solution. "How do I get clients from YouTube with a small channel" is buyer intent. Someone searching that phrase already has a channel, already wants clients, and is actively looking for the mechanism.

A merely interesting topic satisfies curiosity without connecting to a live problem. "The history of YouTube's algorithm" is interesting. Plenty of people will watch it. Almost none of them are one step away from hiring anyone, because the question itself has no money attached to it.

Both kinds of videos can get views. Only one kind reliably gets you customers. If your channel is a mix of both, that is fine, but you should know which videos are doing which job so you are not surprised when the interesting ones do not convert.

## How Do You Actually Validate a Topic Before You Film It?

This is the part most people skip, and it is the entire difference between guessing and knowing. Validation takes ten minutes and it should happen before every single video, not just the big ones.

Start by typing your rough topic into the YouTube search bar itself and stopping before you hit enter. Autocomplete will finish the phrase with what people actually search, not what you assumed they search. If you typed "how to get clients from" and autocomplete suggests "youtube," "linkedin," and "cold email" in that order, you now know exactly how your topic ranks against the alternatives people are considering.

Then run the same rough phrase through a regular Google search and look at the People Also Ask box. Those are real questions real people asked, pulled directly from search data. If three or four of those questions cluster around the same theme as your topic, that is confirmation, not coincidence.

Finally, check whether existing videos on that exact topic have meaningful view counts relative to the channel that posted them. A topic with zero competition sometimes means zero demand, not an untapped opportunity. A topic with a handful of channels all getting solid views on it means there is a real, proven audience actively searching, and there is room for your specific angle inside it.

Do all three checks and you will know, with actual evidence, whether a topic deserves your filming time. Skip them and you are back to guessing dressed up as strategy.

## How Many Topics Should You Keep Validated and Ready?

Validate topics in batches, not one at a time right before you film. The moment you are staring at a blank calendar with a filming slot due tomorrow, you will reach for whatever crosses your mind, and that is exactly the moment buyer intent discipline breaks down.

Keep a running backlog of at least eight to ten validated topics at all times. When you sit down to film, you are choosing from a pre-approved list instead of inventing something under time pressure. This one habit alone fixes most of the topic drift I see on founder channels, where the first ten videos are sharp and buyer intent, and by video thirty the channel has quietly turned into whatever the founder felt like talking about that week.

Sort that backlog by where the viewer is in their decision, too. Some searches come from someone who barely knows they have a problem. Others come from someone actively comparing solutions and close to paying for one. A healthy content mix has both, but if every video you have planned targets someone six months from being ready to buy, your inbox will stay quiet no matter how good the videos are.

## A Worked Example: Validating One Topic Start to Finish

Say your rough idea is "how to make client testimonial videos." Before filming anything, type "how to make client testimonial" into YouTube's search bar and let autocomplete finish it. If it completes with "videos for business" and "for small business," you already know the exact phrasing your audience uses, which might be different from the phrase you started with.

Next, run "client testimonial videos" through Google and look at People Also Ask. If it surfaces "how long should a testimonial video be" and "how do you ask a client for a testimonial," those are two more angles worth covering, either as sections inside your video or as separate videos in the same cluster.

Then check what already exists. If three or four channels have solid, above average views on this exact topic, that confirms real demand and tells you there is room for your specific angle, whether that is your industry, your price point, or your particular method for getting the testimonial in the first place. If literally nothing has been made on it, that is a caution flag, not an opportunity. Zero competition after a real search topic almost never means untapped gold. It usually means nobody is searching.

Ten minutes of checking turned a vague idea into a validated, buyer intent topic with confirmed demand and a specific angle. That is the whole process, repeated for every video before it gets filmed, not just the ones that feel important.

## What Do You Do When the Data and Your Gut Disagree?

Sometimes you will be certain a topic will perform, and the search data will say otherwise. Trust the data. Gut instinct about what your audience wants is really just a guess dressed up with confidence, and it is wrong more often than most founders want to admit, because you know your business far better than you know what a stranger searching YouTube actually needs.

That does not mean your instincts are worthless. Use them to generate the rough list of ideas worth checking. Just do not let them be the final decision on what gets filmed. The check takes ten minutes. Filming a video nobody was searching for costs you an afternoon and a slot in your publishing calendar that a validated topic could have filled instead.
