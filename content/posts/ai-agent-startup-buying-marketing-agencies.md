An AI-agent company just spent up to $25 million buying two marketing agencies. Not software. Not a platform license. The agencies themselves, with their staff and their client books intact. If you run an agency, that detail matters more than the price tag.

## What Actually Happened?

Enso, an AI-agent company founded in 2024 by Mickey Haslavsky (previously a co-founder of RapidAPI), agreed to acquire an Israeli performance marketing agency managing roughly $200 million in media budgets, plus a US-based marketing and go-to-market firm, for a combined $20 to $25 million, according to Calcalist's CTech, reported October 5, 2026. The deal would add around 50 people to Enso's current team of 26. Enso's agents are built to analyze how AI platforms rank, recommend and cite content, test small experiments, and turn whatever works into an ongoing workflow the agents keep running.

## Why Buy the Agencies Instead of Just Selling Them Software?

This is the part worth sitting with. Enso already builds the AI agents. It did not need to acquire anyone to get the technology. It needed the thing technology alone cannot buy quickly: standing client relationships, a signed book of retainers, and the operational trust that comes from an agency already being inside a client's accounts and inbox.

That is the real signal here. The acquisition price is effectively a shortcut past years of sales cycles, cold outreach and proof-of-concept projects that an AI-native company would otherwise have to run one client at a time. Buying two agencies gets Enso roughly 50 people and an existing set of client relationships in one transaction instead of fifty separate ones.

## Does This Mean Agencies Are Becoming Obsolete?

Read the deal the other way and it says almost the opposite. If client relationships, delivery trust and retainer books were worthless once AI agents exist, an AI company would not pay eight figures for two agencies' worth of them. It would just build software and let the agencies wither on their own.

Instead, the agencies' human relationships are explicitly priced as an asset worth acquiring. What's actually happening is a bet that the winning combination is AI agents plus the client trust an agency has already earned, not AI agents instead of that trust.

## Is This Part of a Bigger Pattern?

It fits one. Agencies have spent most of 2026 absorbing rising AI infrastructure costs without a clean way to price them, often bundling token spend into existing retainers rather than billing it as a separate line, while holding companies build their own internal tooling to recoup the same costs at a larger scale. Against that backdrop, an AI-native company buying agencies outright, rather than selling them a tool and hoping they adopt it, is a faster way to control both the technology and the economics of delivery at once. It skips the adoption curve entirely.

That also explains the price. $20 to $25 million for roughly 50 people and an existing book of retainer clients is not a software license fee. It is closer to what it costs to buy years of earned trust with real clients, compressed into a single transaction instead of spread across dozens of sales cycles an AI-native company would otherwise have to run itself, one skeptical prospect at a time.

## What Does This Actually Change If You Run an Agency?

Three things, concretely.

**Your client relationships are not a commodity you should take for granted.** They are apparently worth enough that a well-funded AI company would rather buy them than compete for them. Treat them that way: document them, deepen them, and don't assume they survive on autopilot while you chase new logos.

**The agencies that get bought, or that out-compete a buyer like this, are the ones already running on systems, not just headcount.** An agency whose delivery is still entirely manual hours is a less attractive acquisition and a weaker competitor against one that has already automated its reporting, its content production or its review management. The acquisition math works because Enso is betting it can run those client relationships more efficiently than the agencies currently do alone.

**This is a US and UK/Europe signal, not just an Israeli one.** Enso's stated ambitions are global, and the agency economics behind this deal (thin margins, high AI budget relative to agency size, owner-led sales) apply just as much to a 15-person agency in Austin or Manchester as they do in Tel Aviv.
